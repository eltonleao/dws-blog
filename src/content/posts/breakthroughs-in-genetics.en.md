# Breakthroughs in Genetics

Genetics has moved from reading our DNA to editing it, and the pace keeps rising. Sequencing a whole genome is now fast and affordable, and new tools can correct single errors in the genetic code. Knowing how they work helps you weigh their promise, their limits and the choices they raise.

It begins with reading. The genome is a long text written in four chemical letters, and machines read it by breaking it into fragments, decoding each one and letting software reassemble the whole. The cost has fallen so far that doctors can sequence a seriously ill newborn and find a rare disease in days, where families once waited years. Scientists have also closed the last gaps in the human reference genome and begun adding sequences from people of many ancestries.

Editing came next. The best known tool, CRISPR, borrows a defense system from bacteria: a guide molecule matches a chosen stretch of DNA, and an enzyme cuts it there. The cell repairs the cut, and that repair can switch a gene off or let a corrected version slip in. Newer methods, base editing and prime editing, swap a single letter or rewrite a short passage without cutting both strands.

These tools have already reached patients. One approved treatment for sickle cell disease takes a patient's own blood stem cells, edits them to restart a form of hemoglobin the body normally makes only before birth, and returns them. Gene therapies that deliver a working copy of a gene inside a harmless virus have restored some sight in a form of inherited blindness and changed the outlook for babies with a severe muscle-wasting disease. These treatments are complex and expensive, and patients are followed for years.

Genetics also reaches daily life through home DNA kits and clinical tests. They can reveal ancestry, carrier status or inherited risks, but a result is a probability, not a destiny: most common conditions depend on many genes, plus lifestyle and environment. Before testing, find out who keeps your sample and data, and remember that your results also reveal something about your relatives. For medical questions, a genetic counselor can explain what a variant means and what to do next.
