# Vegan Recipes for Beginners

Vegan cooking is easiest to learn by starting with dishes you already love and swapping a few ingredients. A hearty lentil chili, a chickpea curry and a crispy tofu stir-fry need no special skills, and the tricks behind them, from building savory depth to pressing tofu, carry over to almost any recipe.

A lentil chili is a forgiving first dish. Soften onion, garlic and peppers in oil, stir in cumin, smoked paprika and a spoonful of tomato paste, and let the paste darken briefly so its sweetness deepens. Add brown lentils, canned tomatoes and water, then simmer until the lentils are soft and the pot is thick. Lentils need no soaking, bring both protein and fiber, and taste even better the next day.

Much of what makes meat satisfying is savory flavor, often called umami, and plants can supply it too. Soy sauce, miso, mushrooms, tomato paste and nutritional yeast are rich in the same compounds that make a broth taste full. A chickpea curry shows the idea: fry onion, ginger and curry paste, add chickpeas and coconut milk, and finish with a dash of soy sauce and a squeeze of lime. Fat carries aroma, so a little coconut milk or oil keeps the dish from tasting thin.

Tofu wins over skeptics once it is cooked properly. Choose a firm block, wrap it in a clean towel and press it under something heavy for a while, so the water leaves and the tofu can brown instead of steam. Cut it into cubes, toss them with a little cornstarch and fry until golden on every side. Add them to a stir-fry of broccoli and peppers with soy sauce and garlic only at the end, so they stay crisp.

A few swaps cover baking and breakfast. Ground flaxseed mixed with water and left to thicken replaces an egg in muffins and pancakes, and the liquid from a can of chickpeas whips up like egg white. Over the day, combine legumes with whole grains, nuts and seeds. Since plant foods lack one essential B vitamin, rely on fortified foods or a supplement, and ask your doctor which suits you.
